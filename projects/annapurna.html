The Annapurna Circuit

The Annapurna Circuit is a long-distance trek in Nepal that loops around the Annapurna massif. The full route is about 230 km, depending on where you start and finish and whether you use transport. It is a teahouse trek, so you sleep and eat in simple guesthouses along the way, with modest rooms, basic facilities and plenty of dal bhat.

The circuit is known for big altitude shifts, quiet valleys, and lived-in villages with Gurung and Tibetan influences, all set against Annapurna and Dhaulagiri. Side trips to high lakes are common, including Ice Lake and Tilicho. The defining feature is Thorung La Pass at 5,416 m, the highest trekkable mountain pass in the world, which gives the route its reputation for serious but achievable adventure.

Our Trek, the Stats and the Trail

This page is a geospatial and analytical take on my Annapurna Circuit trek. It pulls together the bits I found most interesting, ties the numbers to the places they happened, all while including some accounts of memorable events.

It includes interactive route maps alongside clear, static visuals that pin key moments and metrics to place. The aim is to pair location with numbers so the trek reads as a story, not a pile of stats.

Day 1: Dharaphani to Chame

First things first, we checked in at the ACAP checkpoint with our permits before beginning the trek. There are a couple of options to start the day:
- Cross the river and follow the walking trail through Thonche, or
- Follow the road and take the blue/white marked route through Odar

We chose the latter, as all advice from the officers at the permit office was that it would not be possible to get back on the main trail (although I regrettably found out later that the trail crosses back and rejoins the road at Bagarchhap).

  • Dharaphani (1 860m) - Timang (2 750m) - Chame (2 670m)

  • Distance: 17.4km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 1 080m
  • Moving Time: 7h 52m
The area is alpine and, during our time there in late September, the low-lying clouds made for some intense views, with the peaks almost completely hidden by the clouds.

As mentioned in the overview section, that little man in a cast marks the point at which I slipped on a rock and fell into a stream, breaking my hand and bashing my shin open. Luckily, Spike had some bandages for my leg, and I thankfully did not need my hand to walk.

You cover most of the day's elevation in one swoop, climbing up a winding staircase, but once you are at the top it is pretty much smooth sailing. We stopped in Timang for lunch and to assess the injuries before making our way to Chame, where we stayed at the New Tibet Hotel & Restaurant.

Day 2: Chame to Upper Pisang

After a stop at the local health post in Chame, where an x-ray from a machine that looked like it came out of the Soviet era confirmed a non-displaced fracture and my leg was wrapped up, we were on our way. Most of the day's trail follows the road, but the views definitely make up for it, as we got our first glimpse of snow-covered peaks in the early hours of the morning before they were swallowed up by the clouds.

  • Chame (2 670m) - Dhukur Pokhari (3 190m) - Upper Pisang (3 200m)

  • Distance: 14.9km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 851m
  • Moving Time: 6h 34m
On our way, we made a pit stop at an apple farm in Bhratang, grabbing some freshly squeezed juice before heading off. Again, the pine forests did not align at all with my expectations of Nepal, and Spike and I agreed they reminded us more of Europe. We continued the climb upwards until the path evened out near the village of Dhukur Pokhari, which has some incredible views of Swarga Dwar, a dramatic cliff face known as the Gateway to Heaven.

After lunch, we made the final push up to Upper Pisang, where we stayed in the Alpine Hotel. It is important to note that Lower and Upper Pisang are the first places where it is possible to start experiencing altitude sickness, just ask Spike. Drink plenty of water and even more garlic soup to stay on top of things.

Day 3: Upper Pisang to Ghyaru

After hearing incredible things about the views of Annapurna from Ghyaru, and it being one of the more traditional villages on the circuit, I decided to stay the night. This made for a relatively short day of walking, although the steep trek up to Ghyaru should not be underestimated. Since we had arrived so early, we took it as an opportunity to catch up on some sleep and play some backgammon, after which I went for a walk up the mountain to one of the monuments.

  • Upper Pisang (3 200m) - Ghyaru (3 730m)

  • Distance: 8.1km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 734m
  • Moving Time: 6h 50m
Unfortunately, the weather was not in our favour, as it had been pouring since the late morning, so those amazing views were absent. Due to Ghyaru being one of the more traditional villages, there are limited teahouse options, none of which during our stay had hot water. So if that is a deal breaker, rather carry on through Ghyaru to Ngawal and then Manang, or alternatively, from Upper Pisang, take the road all the way to Manang. That said, we still appreciated the break, and proceeding through to Manang after Ghyaru would not have done Spike's altitude sickness any favours.

Day 4: Ghyaru to Manang


After searching for our teahouse host for the better part of the early morning, we eventually got our apple and oats and started our trek to Manang. Today was our worst day in terms of weather, as the rain from the previous morning had developed into a constant and uncomfortable downpour. Thankfully, from Ghyaru it is a relatively straightforward but long walk all the way to Manang. The trail winds through Ngawal and then eventually heads through the forest, rejoining the road near Braga.

  • Ghyaru (3 730m) - Manang (3 540m)

  • Distance: 15.1km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 326m
  • Moving Time: 4h 46m
Feeling fresh after having a relatively chilled day prior, we decided to skip lunch and trudge through the rain, hoods up and hardly speaking, but still receiving the hourly interjection from me to Spike saying, "Bru, how awesome is this," and Spike’s favourite response, "This f**king sucks." Although we had some pretty serious cloud cover, the views on this part of the trek are known to be some of the best, so my imagination was running wild and I was itching to see Annapurna II in all her 7 937m glory.

Manang is one of the more developed villages and has plenty of options for teahouses. We stayed in Tilicho Hotel, which was easily the best place we stayed on the circuit. It has Western toilets in some bathrooms, hot showers and awesome hangout spaces. It was also where both Spike and I stopped our vegetarian streak and opted for the yak burger, which we would both highly recommend.

Manang also has one of the last decently equipped health posts. I made my way over there upon arrival to see if they could do anything, like create a new splint for my hand, seeing as the one made in Chame went all the way up to my elbow and made putting on clothes a nightmare. Although it was a public holiday in mourning for those who had lost their lives in the protests that had only just finished, they kindly sawed about 10cm off my cast, which made life slightly easier.

Day 5: Day Hike to Ice Lake

It is very popular for trekkers to spend an extra day in Manang to help them get acclimatised to the altitude, and Spike and I decided to do the same. There are many options for the rest day, including actually resting, or a range of hikes that will help you prep for the days ahead. Spike and I opted for the hike up to the Ice Lake (Kicho Tal).

  • Manang (3 540m) - Ice Lake (4 600m) - Manang (3 540m)

  • Distance: 16.7km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 1 102m
  • Moving Time: 7h 38m
After our standard breakfast of oats, apple and honey, and reconciling our snack supplies, we set off to the Ice Lake, sharing one day pack. After retracing our steps to Braga (the village from the previous day), we began to make our way up the zig-zagging trail. As we ascended, the cloud cover began to thin and Annapurna II towered over us in all her white-peaked glory. This was our first real encounter with an almost 8 000m peak, and we were stoked. As we continued up, we passed a small teahouse where hikers stop to take a breath. We joined in, tucking into our leftover biltong and trail mix.

It was after the teahouse that the altitude really gripped me. As we continued to ascend, I felt light-headed and nauseous, but still strong enough to continue. We made the final push to the lake and I must admit that it was totally worth feeling my heartbeat in my eyes. I swiftly stripped to the Speedo I had brought along and made my way into the water for a quick dip and photo (just for the sake of it), had a brief stone-skipping competition (which, like the backgammon, I won), and then made our way back down the switchbacks.

Although many treat the extra day in Manang as an acclimatisation day, the hike up to the Ice Lake was the most elevation gain we had covered in a day and, through a combination of altitude sickness and getting pretty sunburnt on the mountain, paired with my existing injuries, I was buggered. I can only recommend drinking plenty of water, not being an idiot and forgetting the sun cream, and taking the ascent easily. From here on out though, I had no issues with altitude.

Day 6: Manang to Tilicho Base Camp

To Spike's dismay, we were leaving the safety of our favourite teahouse. After paying for our meals, we made a late start for Tilicho Base Camp. This is not officially part of the circuit, although I was set on seeing Tilicho Lake, one of the highest lakes in the world.

  • Manang (3 540m) - Shri Kharka (4 000m) - Tilicho Base Camp (4 150m)

  • Distance: 15.3km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 956m
  • Moving Time: 7h 28m
The weather had definitely taken a turn for the better, as our visibility was significantly improved. After following the road from Manang, we slowly made our way up to Shri Kharka for lunch, where we both tucked into spring rolls, which we regretted hugely. For the sake of the audience, I will leave out the details of why. After Shri Kharka we reached the most technical part of the trek.

We continued along a metre-wide footpath made of very loose shale fragments that did us no favours in gaining any footing, and which was littered with signs warning of landslides, as well as a 300 m drop to a riverbed below, all while sitting at a cool altitude of 4 300 m. Although I was pretty nervous walking along, with no pole on my left and the cliff side, the donkeys and locals that we crossed paths with clearly did not share our concerns as they paced along the path.

Once we turned the corner of the mountain we were walking around, we finally had base camp in our sight, a huge relief to both of us. We quickly got a room at New Tilicho Hotel, had an early dal bhat dinner, and Spike had a watery garlic soup. We had an early night, as the next day we were cracking the 5 000 m altitude milestone.

Day 7: Day Hike to Tilicho Lake

Although our start time does not reflect it, given some righteous faffing while packing the day pack, we had an early wake-up to grab breakfast and make our way to Tilicho Lake. As soon as I came out of the room, I was slapped with the jaw-dropping view of Khangsar, with its sharp peak and wide shoulders towering over base camp. After our breakfast, coffee and a very distracted attempt at journalling the previous day, given that I could not take my eyes off Khangsar, we packed the shared day pack and set off to Tilicho Lake.

  • Tilicho Base Camp (4 150m) - Tilicho Lake (4 920m) - Tilicho Base Camp (4 150m)

  • Distance: 15.3km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 900m
  • Moving Time: 5h 38m
Like the day hike to the Ice Lake, Tilicho Lake comprised a straight ascent up switchbacks to the top, all the while being surrounded by some of my favourite views of the trek. As we made our way up, just shy of 5 000m was the most glorious dog, which became a more distracting sight than the mountains, as trekkers adored how relaxed this dog was while we were all huffing and puffing our way up the switchbacks.

If my expectations were set by the Ice Lake, then they were blown completely out of the water (excuse the pun) by the deafeningly blue water and surrounding behemoths of Tilicho Peak and Nilgiri Himal. The viewpoints around the lake were relatively empty, so we had some decent peace and quiet and could hear echoes of the glaciers shifting under the weight of themselves, bouncing off the lake. Once we had taken in the view, we made our way down to Base Camp, as Spike was having a mild panic seeing as he had forgotten his water bottle and we had finished my stock.

Upon arrival at the teahouse, we had the better part of the day left, so got stuck into a couple of Gorkha beers, and I inspected the gash in my shin that was not in a good state and had begun to get infected. Not good news, seeing as we were two days from a medical post. Thankfully, Spike once again came to my rescue and, being the medically stocked angel he was, had a general antibiotic. I ended the day with a bowl of thukpa soup, and as my journal reads, "craved a Woolies reheatable meal."

Day 8: Tilicho Base Camp to Yak Kharka

We made our way out of Tilicho Base Camp, retracing our steps along the stunning but most technical part of the trail as we headed back to Shri Kharka, where we had our regrettable spring rolls. This time was far better for me, considering I now had a pole between myself and the drop-off. We encountered a mountain biker on the way who had insanely cycled from Manang up to Tilicho Lake and was on his way back, an awesome free-wheel down, but he had covered some 2 000m+ of elevation in about 30km.

  • Tilicho Base Camp (4 150m) - Churilatter Suspension Bridge (3 830m) - Yak Kharka (4 100m)

  • Distance: 17.2km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 779m
  • Moving Time: 8h 14m
Once we arrived in Shri Kharka, instead of taking the road to Manang, we took the high trail that traced up the mountainside and headed toward Gunsang. From here it was pretty much downhill, and the grass, blue sky, white peaks and bare rock made for some breathtaking views, with Himalayan griffons crossing the tree line. As we descended into Gunsang, we approached a bridge with a small teahouse where we decided to grab lunch, joining another group of trekkers from Denmark. As if we had not learned already, we tucked into a spring roll lunch yet again.

After our meal, and Spike fuelling his Fanta addiction, we made the final ascent up to Yak Kharka, not before encountering a yak on the path that was in no rush to let us past. Upon arrival, we checked into the Dream Home teahouse, where we met Martin, a German also doing the trek with his sherpa. We shared our vegetable fried rice as Martin told us about his travels over the past year, which included trekking practically every continent. He was following a similar path to us on the trek but was staying at High Camp rather than our destination, Thorung Phedi. Once we had finished dinner, we put our orders in for breakfast and headed for an early night. Tomorrow was the final stop before the big day.

Day 9: Yak Kharka to Thorung Phedi

Our ninth day on the Circuit was the shortest day and our final day before attempting to cross the 5 416 m Thorung-La Pass. Making our way up to Thorung Phedi meant that we would be going above the recommended 500 metres for sleeping, but regardless of the option you choose, it will be the highest you will sleep on the circuit. At this altitude, you are only getting around 55% of the oxygen you would get at sea level, so it is entirely normal to feel more out of breath than usual. It is just important to stay hydrated and, as the locals recommend, eat plenty of garlic soup.

  • Yak Kharka (4 100m) - Thorung Phedi (4 450m)

  • Distance: 6.8km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 555m
  • Moving Time: 2h 38m
After our brief trek, we arrived in Thorung Phedi, which is not so much a village as just a few guesthouses where a lot of trekkers choose to spend their last night before attempting the pass. The only disadvantage to staying in Thorung Phedi is that it makes for a very early wake-up to start the trek, as we were up at 3 a.m. However, I would still recommend it, seeing as High Camp is notorious for terrible sleep because of the altitude.

We spent the rest of our day in Thorung Phedi drinking ginger tea, playing backgammon and chatting nonsense about the trek so far. Once it hit 7 p.m., we had already had dinner, put our orders in for breakfast and were ready to head to bed before the big day.

Day 10: Thorung Phedi to Muktinath

Our tenth day on the trek was one of the most incredible. We had a surprisingly easy wake-up at 3 a.m. and wolfed down our eggs, toast and yak butter before packing our bags and beginning the trek. I was itching to get out the door to get ahead of the other trekkers and reach sunrise close to the top, but Spike had other ideas, packing his bag particularly slowly that day. Once he was packed, I was out the door with my headlamp on, staring at my feet and making small, consistent progress up the switchbacks to High Camp.

  • Thorung Phedi (4 450m) - Thorung La Pass (5 416m) - Phedi Restaurant (3 990m) - Muktinath (3 670m)

  • Distance: 16.6km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 868m
  • Moving Time: 8h 59m
I had gotten a bit ahead of myself and had passed through High Camp before I took a true account of where Spike was behind me. I met a girl from Catalonia who exclaimed that I was the first lone trekker like herself she had come across on the circuit. I had to quickly deny that claim and just replied that I was not a very good trekking partner in this particular case. After chatting to her, I waited until Spike thankfully came around the corner swearing at me for leaving him. Sorry Spike.

Now together, we continued our ascent with the sun peeking over the mountains, drenching everything in its warm glow. The visibility that day was some of the best we had, and we were finally at a level where we did not have to break our necks to look up at Annapurna II, whose peak was still about 2 400 m higher than we currently were. Wild to think. We continued climbing, and sooner than expected we headed over a small hill and were met by the view of a typical Annapurna Circuit yellow, black and blue sign covered in prayer flags and, just like that, we were at Thorung-La Pass.

Spike and I gave each other a huge hug, forgetting the morning’s argument, and shared in the excitement of the achievement we had just made. We took our photos, gave out some high-fives to some Austrian trekkers, and began the 1 700m descent towards Muktinath. As if borrowed, the relief of reaching the pass was swiftly gone as the descent tested what was left of our knee cartilage.

We eventually stopped at Phedi Restaurant in Mustang, joining the Austrian girls for lunch, as Spike and I could not stop speaking about the congratulatory beer we were going to have in Muktinath. From here the descent became slightly more bearable on the final stretch, which eventually reached the staircase before the village and revealed a mountainside covered in tens of thousands of prayer flags blowing in the wind. Before arriving at our Mustang Hotel teahouse, we walked around the Hindu shrine and came across a ceremony where people were taking part in prayer and jumping into fountains around a temple.

We had our celebratory Gorkha beers, hot showers and rotted in bed for the rest of the day. Our final trekking day was tomorrow, and it was all downhill from here, in a good way.

Day 11: Muktinath to Jomsom

There are a variety of options for getting to Jomsom, including buses and jeeps that follow the main road on a 1.5 to 2 hour journey. There is also a walking trail that travels through Lubra and cuts a big section of road. However, since it was our last day trekking on the circuit, we were keen to check out one of the most traditional villages, Kagbeni, which is reached via a relatively new paved road out of Muktinath.

  • Muktinath (3 670m) - Kagbeni (2 810m) - Jomsom (2 830m)

  • Distance: 22.7km
  • Total Elevation Gain: 401m
  • Moving Time: 7h 53m
After a glorious breakfast (the food quality gets better the lower you are), we set off, following the path through small villages and a winding route that passed fields, terraces and streams. Once we were well out of Muktinath, the landscape became significantly more arid, with hardly any vegetation and a few mountain goats. After a while we joined back onto the paved road, descending down a steep, carved section that dropped right into Kagbeni, which is filled with far more traditional architecture than we had seen on the circuit, as well as the Kag Chode Thupten Samphei Ling Monastery. We eventually found refuge for lunch in Yacdonalds, obviously ordering their version of a Big Mac, the “Big Yac.”

Once we had finished lunch, the wind had picked up significantly. Mustang is notorious for dry winds at this time of year, picking up all the dust in the region and making visibility pretty difficult. The wind only got worse as we continued, with the fine dust even managing to get past our buffs and sunglasses. This part of the trek was not fun. Eventually, I made the call to get off the road and take our chances by cutting along a dried-out, wide riverbed that ran near the road, as it would cut off roughly a kilometre. First mistake. The dust was far worse here and we were in a dust storm.

We tried to make our way at a faster pace, but eventually found that we were cut off by a small river that we would have to cross. We assessed the best place to pass through, but there was no way we were not getting our boots soaked. Spike, now pissed off at having wet boots, trudged forward as I wrung out my socks. We continued on this for about 3 km until we realised that we had again been cut off by another river that we were going to have to cross to get back to the final stretch before Jomsom. It was laughable at this point. Getting our boots wet once again, we made our way along the road leading into Jomsom.

Dirty, tired and starving, we finally arrived in Jomsom and our mood was immediately lifted by a warm shower and some celebratory beers at our accommodation, Orms Hotel, to commemorate the end of the trek. I smashed my final, and favourite, dal bhat of the circuit and we laughed at our misfortune of the day.

The next day we were to bus to Tatopani, where we would make the executive call to abandon the trek up to Poon Hill and rather soak in the hot springs and finally do some relaxing. After our stay in Tatopani, we would make our final stop in Pokhara before catching a flight back to Kathmandu and then Cape Town.
